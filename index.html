01 · The machine private equity actually runs

Its fortune was never about what it bought. It was about how it was built.

The returns that built private equity do not come from picking better companies. They come from a structure: acquire an asset using mostly borrowed money, let the asset's own cash flow retire that debt, compound the equity through value creation, and — at exit — own a de-levered, larger asset for a fraction of what it is now worth. Most of the return is manufactured by the structure, not the selection.

A structure can be copied. A secret cannot. Private equity is a structure — and it has been hiding in plain sight, behind a velvet rope marked institutional access only.

And it isn't just private equity. Every durable fortune runs this same machine.

Borrow at a reasonable rate · let cash flow carry the debt · compound the rest. Look closely and the same three ingredients are everywhere.

The U.S. government

Borrows at the lowest rates on earth (Treasuries), services it with tax revenue, and compounds the economy it borrows against. It has run on debt for most of its history and grown richer, not poorer — leverage in service of a compounding base.

Banks

Borrow at near-zero — your deposits — lend and invest at higher rates, and compound the spread. The entire business is cheap leverage + cash flow + compounding, wearing a different name.

Berkshire & insurers

Insurance "float" is borrowed money at near-zero or negative cost — premiums held before claims — invested and compounded for decades. Buffett's real edge was never stock-picking; it was cheap, permanent leverage.

Homeowners

A mortgage is cheap, 30-year leverage on an appreciating asset; income carries the payment; equity compounds as the loan amortizes beneath it. The most common wealth-builder in America — the same machine, retail-sized.

University endowments

The Yale model: take a fixed cash-flow draw, compound the rest in high-return assets, lever prudently — and do it for centuries. Permanence is just the machine, left running.

Private equity

Buy with mostly debt, let the company's cash flow retire it, compound the equity through value creation, own a de-levered and larger asset at exit. The template this page is named for.

Same three ingredients, every time: borrow at a reasonable rate, throw off cash flow to carry it, and compound the rest. That combination — not a job, not a salary — is what actually buys freedom. The machine was never the secret. The only thing that ever varied was access. Synthetic PE is that access — the same three ingredients, rebuilt with liquid instruments, on your own balance sheet.

▸ The number that actually builds wealth

You were taught to chase ROI. The machine is built on ROE.

Return on investment asks how hard your cash works — supply the whole amount, let it grow, hope it outruns inflation. Return on equity asks how hard a small slice of your own capital works once it controls a far larger position. Borrow at a reasonable rate, let the cash-flow engine carry the debt, let the compounding engine build the equity — and the same dollar of yours does the work of many. That is the whole move synthetic PE makes.

▸ Why the return is on your equity

Put down a slice. The asset compounds on its full value — and the whole gain is yours.

Leverage lets a small downpayment control a much larger asset. The asset compounds on its full value — its growth has nothing to do with the loan — while the borrowed principal amortizes away on its own schedule. Because no loan growth offsets the gain, the entire appreciation lands on your equity: a small amount in becomes a large net equity out. That ratio is your return on equity.

Small downpayment · the asset compounds on its full value · the loan amortizes independently · illustrative

You put down a small slice — the downpayment. Just as a home appreciates on its full price while the mortgage only shrinks, the asset compounds on its full value, unrelated to the loan, which amortizes independently to zero. Because no loan growth offsets the gain, the entire appreciation becomes your net equity — a large equity out on a small equity in. That is return on equity (ROE). ▸ The record

You have already run this machine — on a house. Here is the completed record.

Everything above this point is a diagram. This is not. Below are two California mortgages taken from the same thirty years of real index data and run through identical arithmetic to opposite conclusions. One turned $35,454 into +$444,810. The other destroyed $365,601. Same asset, same structure, same fixed-rate amortizing loan. The only variable that differed is the one this entire business is built around — whether the owner could keep paying, and was never forced to sell.

The California house, 1996 to today

The situation

1996. The statewide median California home costs $177,270 and you have $35,454 — not a fortune, a fifth of a house. Thirty-year money costs 7.89%, which everyone that year agrees is expensive. You buy anyway, because in 1996 that is simply what one does.

The move

You put up a fifth and a lender puts up the rest, fixed, for thirty years. Then the structure asks only two things of you: make the payment, and do not sell. The house compounds on the entire $177,270. You compound on your $35,454. That gap between the two bases is the whole fortune.

California houses did not make anyone rich by rising 5.37% a year. Amortized debt did, by letting a fifth of the money own all of the asset for thirty years.
Why it worked anyway

Notice what did not happen here. The money cost 7.89% and the house compounded at 5.37%. The loan was more expensive than the asset was productive — every year, for thirty years — and it still returned twenty-four times the capital at risk. The spread was never the engine. Five dollars of asset compounding on one dollar of yours, against a balance that fell every month for three decades: that was the engine.

The situation

2006. The same statewide-median house now costs $555,986 — the index has more than tripled since 1996. Money is cheaper than the 1996 buyer paid: 6.41%. Everything the first buyer did right, this buyer does too. A fifth down. Thirty-year fixed. Make the payment. Hold the asset.

What happened

Then, in 2008, the income stops. By mid-2011 the index has fallen from 638 to 385 — about 40%. The house is worth $335,295. You have paid $144,911 in interest and retired only $30,550 of principal, so you still owe $414,238. Selling does not release capital; it crystallises a debt.

The 1996 buyer was never forced to sell. This one was. That is the entire difference between +$444,810 and −$365,601.
And the one who could keep paying

The same 2006 buyer who held still owns the house. Twenty years in it is worth $850,971 against a $246,271 balance — $604,700 of equity, still $174,919 behind on cash in, with ten years left to run. They bought at the worst moment in California history, at 6.41% money against an asset that has compounded at just 2.15% a year since — the worst spread on this page — and they are recovering. Not because they were right. Because they were never forced to be.

Two identical machines. The one that survived had a reserve.

Put the two side by side and the conclusion is unavoidable. The leverage was not the risk. Both buyers used the same leverage, and the one who paid the higher rate against the slower asset made $444,810. The risk was being a forced seller — and what makes someone a forced seller is never the asset. It is running out of the cash that services the debt.

That is the whole reason this machine is built the way it is: the income sleeve exists to cover the carry so the position is never liquidated to make a payment, and the reserve is funded first so a losing stretch is survivable rather than terminal. Case 2 is what the reserve is for. It is not a conservatism tax — it is the difference between the two rows above.

Across the full term your average outstanding balance is only —. You are never billed on the full amount except on day one. That is why the loan behaves like a cheaper one than the paperwork says.

2 · The asset does not shrink

A protected base has no drawdown and no recovery years. Every year compounds on a base at least as large as the year before — so growth is charged on a rising number while interest is charged on a falling one.

Interest stops at the final payment and is frozen forever. The base keeps going. Every year after payoff is growth against a cost that can no longer increase — that is where the record above comes from.

02 · The same structure, rebuilt synthetically

You will not buy a company. You will run the same three-part machine on instruments you can exit in seconds.

Each part of the private-equity machine has a synthetic counterpart — built from instruments whose downside is defined to the dollar before you ever take a position. Two engines do the work. They are presented together as one architecture, and their records are kept rigorously separate.

Part 01 · Leverage, rebuilt

Engineered position — not borrowed money.

In a buyout, leverage is bank debt. Synthetically, "leverage" is the capital efficiency of futures and options: exposure that is a fraction of the capital sitting behind it, with risk capped at entry. The architecture deploys a small working unit against a far larger risk-capital buffer held in reserve — so a single position is always a sliver of the capital, never the whole of it. Adherence-protected, not cushion-protected. This is not an instruction to take on debt; it is a way to control exposure with defined risk.

05 · The difference between a structure and a story

Most people who touch these instruments lose. We tell you that — then show you our own math, live.

The cemetery is full of people who ran convex instruments without an architecture. What separates this is not a better story. It is a disclosed, locked, witnessed structure — and a public commitment to the exact conditions under which the operator admits the edge is gone.

Disclosed & locked

Every parameter is published in full — sizing, caps, stand-downs, the doubling ladder. Not under operator discretion. Not modified under stress. The same on a winning week as a losing one.

Named falsifiability

The conditions under which the experiment ends are written down first — a rolling-100-trade expectancy gate, operationalized in code, that halts the strategy if the edge crosses into negative territory.

Kill criteria fire on reality

Daily, weekly, and monthly stand-downs and auto-revert fire on realized losses, not predictions. The architecture never has to forecast the regime — a bad regime produces losses, and losses trip the brakes.

Witnessed, not self-graded

Protocol changes require a 48-hour cool-off and an independent witness countersignature. The experiment is not over because the operator says so — it is over because the data crossed the line.

Personal capital, every fill

Operator risk capital on every trade. Not pooled. Not managed. Not shared. Every fill posted in public before exit, downloadable and verifiable against live markets.

Honest about the base rate

We publish where retail aggregates actually end up on these instruments. We are not hiding the failure rate — we are naming it, and then putting our own record next to it.
